Rock paper scissors strategy: how to win more with 10 cards

In normal rock paper scissors, "strategy" mostly means guessing well. Give each player a 10-card hand and it becomes a real game of counting, bluffing and timing. Here's how to get better at it.

New to the game? Start with how to play: each player has 3 of two kinds and 4 of the third, plays one card a round for 10 rounds, and every card is gone once played.

1. Know what the hand tells you

Before a single card is played, you already know a lot. Your opponent holds 3, 3 and 4 of rock, paper and scissors in some order. That means:

  • Every kind starts at 3 or 4 in their hand.
  • Only one kind can be their four.
  • Once they've played four of a kind, that was their four, and the other two kinds each started at three.

The "Opp has" tracker in the game shows these ranges as they change. Glance at it every round: it's the single most useful thing on the screen.

2. Count, every round

Counting is the core skill. Keep track of how many of each kind your opponent has played, and think in terms of what's left rather than what's gone. After five rounds, a hand that started 3 rock, 3 paper, 4 scissors and has played 2 rock, 1 paper and 2 scissors still holds 1 rock, 2 paper and 2 scissors. That tells you paper and scissors are their likely plays, and rock is nearly spent.

You won't always know their exact split early on, because you don't know which kind is their four. But every round narrows it down, and by the last three rounds you can often name their remaining cards.

3. The safe card trick

If your opponent has run out of one kind, there's a card you can play that can never lose.
  • They have no rock left → play scissors. It beats their paper and draws their scissors.
  • They have no paper left → play rock. It beats their scissors and draws their rock.
  • They have no scissors left → play paper. It beats their rock and draws their paper.

The rule: play the card their missing kind would have beaten. It's the strongest move in the game, so spend your cards with it in mind. Making your opponent run out of a kind early, or making sure you still hold the safe card when they do, wins a lot of rounds.

It works both ways. If you run out of a kind, your opponent gets a safe card against you. Try not to empty one kind long before the others unless you're getting something for it.

4. Hide your four

Your four is your biggest asset and your biggest tell. If you open with it twice in a row, a counting opponent will soon know what it is and plan around it. Some ideas:

  • Mix it in. Play your four about as often as your other kinds in the early rounds, so your split stays hidden.
  • Hold it back. Keeping two of your four for the final rounds gives you options when everything else is forced.
  • Use it as bait. Once they think they know your four, they'll play to beat it. That's the moment to switch.

5. Read your opponent

People are not random, and RPS 10 is played against people. Studies of rock paper scissors have found that players often stick with a choice that just won and switch after a loss, usually to the card that would have beaten what they just lost to. Watch for these habits:

  • Win-stay, lose-shift. If they won with rock, expect rock again (as long as they have one).
  • Opening with the four. Many players lead with their strongest kind. If their first two cards match, that's a likely four.
  • Panic at the clock. Players who lock in late tend to grab their most plentiful kind.

Combine habits with counting: a habit only matters if they still have the card to follow it.

6. Plan the opening, middle and end

Rounds 1–3: gather information

Early rounds are cheap. You know little, they know little, and any card is reasonable. Use them to learn: what do they open with, do they repeat, do they react to your plays? Avoid showing your four twice.

Rounds 4–7: start counting hard

Ranges tighten. Look for a kind they're about to run out of, and keep the card that would become safe against it. Note which kinds you are low on, because they're counting too.

Rounds 8–10: solve it

With three cards left each, you can often work out their exact hand. Plan your last three as a set: which order wins the most rounds against their remaining cards? In round 10, both players have one card. If you've counted correctly, you already know theirs, and the result was decided by how you played rounds 8 and 9.

7. Don't lose rounds to the clock

You have 10 seconds a round. In Ranked, a missed turn loses the round and throws away a random card, which can wreck your count. Make a decision, lock in, and save your thinking time for the late rounds where it matters.

8. Ranked tips

  • Play many opponents. Rating gains against the same player shrink with every rematch in a day, so variety climbs faster.
  • Mind the score, not just the round. A drawn round keeps your lead. When you're ahead late, a safe card that can only win or draw is often the right choice.
  • Take a break after a tilt. Losing streaks make people predictable. Opponents notice.

9. Practise smart

Play a few Practice matches against the bot to drill counting until it's automatic, try the daily challenge (everyone gets the same hand, so it's a fair test), and review the Turns panel after a match to see exactly how each round went.

Quick checklist

  1. Check the "Opp has" tracker every round.
  2. Count what's left, not what's gone.
  3. Out of a kind? Play the card that kind would have beaten.
  4. Don't show your four too early.
  5. Win-stay, lose-shift: expect it, exploit it.
  6. Plan rounds 8–10 together.
  7. Lock in on time.
